# ML Surrogate Models as Priors in Deterministic Ensemble Kalman Filter: A Benchmark Study on the Lorenz-63 System

**Date:** 2026-04-14  
**Framework:** Deterministic ETKF (no inflation, no localization)  
**System:** Lorenz-63 (Nx = 3, dt = 0.01)  
**Ensemble pool:** 50 members, 1000-step spinup per architecture  

---

## Abstract

This report evaluates five machine learning surrogate architectures — Dense Neural Network (DenseNN), Residual Dense Neural Network (ResDenseNN), Long Short-Term Memory Network (LSTMNN), RNN with ReLU activation (RNN_relu), and RNN with tanh activation (RNN_tanh) — as forward dynamical models within a deterministic Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter (ETKF) applied to the Lorenz-63 system. All models were assessed in their raw form: no covariance inflation, no localization, and no post-hoc tuning. Across five stress benchmarks varying forecast window length (M), ensemble size (Ne), observation coverage (p), and observation noise (σ_obs), none of the surrogates induced filter divergence, demonstrating that all five architectures preserve sufficient ensemble variance for stable DA cycling. Key findings are: (1) all surrogates are over-dispersive relative to their own forecast error (spread/fRMSE > 1.0), which provides natural protection against filter collapse; (2) RNN_relu is the weakest performer under most conditions, particularly at low observation accuracy; (3) RNN_tanh and LSTMNN are the most consistent and reliable surrogates; (4) under sparse or noisy observations, some surrogates *outperform* the true Lorenz solver, suggesting that forecast model error can be dominated by observation quality in this regime; and (5) ensemble calibration (spread ≈ RMSE) degrades for all models once the forecast window exceeds the system's effective predictability horizon.

---

## 1. Experimental Setup

### 1.1 Ground Truth
The true system trajectory is generated by integrating the Lorenz-63 equations (σ=10, ρ=28, β=8/3) with a high-accuracy numerical solver. Synthetic observations are created by adding Gaussian noise N(0, σ_obs²) to the true state at each DA cycle.

### 1.2 Surrogate Architectures
All five models were trained as single time-step predictors (dt=0.01) on Lorenz-63 trajectories:

| Architecture | Key property |
|---|---|
| **DenseNN** | Standard feed-forward MLP; memoryless |
| **ResDenseNN** | MLP with residual (skip) connections; memoryless |
| **LSTMNN** | LSTM with gated memory; context-aware |
| **RNN_relu** | Vanilla RNN with ReLU activation; risk of saturation |
| **RNN_tanh** | Vanilla RNN with tanh activation; bounded activations |

### 1.3 DA Framework
The **deterministic ETKF** (Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter) is used throughout. The analysis ensemble is updated via a symmetric square-root transform with no perturbations. **No multiplicative inflation** (factor = 1.0) and **no localization** are applied in any benchmark. This is intentional: we evaluate the raw, unassisted performance of each architecture.

### 1.4 Benchmark Suite

| Benchmark | Purpose | Parameter swept | Fixed settings |
|---|---|---|---|
| **B1 — Baseline** | Reference performance | — | T=10, M=10, Ne=20, p=1.0, σ=1.0 |
| **B2 — Forecast window** | Sensitivity to chaotic growth | M ∈ {5,10,20,40,50,100} | T=50, Ne=20, p=1.0, σ=1.0 |
| **B3 — Ensemble size** | Sampling error sensitivity | Ne ∈ {5,10,20,50} | T=50, M=10, p=1.0, σ=1.0 |
| **B4 — Obs. coverage** | Partial observability stress | p ∈ {1.0, 0.67, 0.33} | T=50, M=10, Ne=20, σ=1.0 |
| **B5 — Obs. noise** | Signal-to-noise sensitivity | σ ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0} | T=50, M=10, Ne=20, p=1.0 |

For all benchmarks, the true trajectory is integrated with the exact Lorenz solver; only the *forecast* step inside the ETKF uses the surrogate.

## 2. Benchmark 1 — Baseline Performance

**Configuration:** T=10 cycles, M=10 forecast steps, Ne=20 members, p=1.0 (all 3 variables observed), σ_obs=1.0.

### 2.1 Numerical Results

| Model | fRMSE | aRMSE | Spread | Spread/fRMSE | Diverged |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lorenz63** | 1.3494 | 0.6590 | 1.2590 | **0.933** | No |
| DenseNN | 1.0022 | 0.6841 | 1.4370 | 1.434 | No |
| ResDenseNN | 0.9727 | 0.6543 | 1.3433 | 1.381 | No |
| LSTMNN | 0.9375 | 0.6603 | 1.1299 | **1.205** | No |
| RNN_relu | 0.8601 | 0.6725 | 1.1852 | 1.378 | No |
| RNN_tanh | 0.8818 | 0.6578 | 1.3043 | 1.479 | No |

### 2.2 Observations

**All models complete 10 DA cycles without divergence.** The analysis RMSE (aRMSE) values are remarkably similar across architectures (0.654–0.684), indicating that in this low-stress regime the ETKF is robust to model choice: the observation system provides enough constraint to correct the forecast errors of all surrogates.

The **forecast RMSE (fRMSE) is lower for all surrogates than for Lorenz63** (0.86–1.00 vs. 1.35). This may appear paradoxical — why does the true model have higher fRMSE? With only T=10 short cycles, the surrogates have not yet revealed their long-range failure modes. More importantly, the Lorenz solver propagates the *chaotic divergence* between ensemble members faithfully, resulting in a larger ensemble spread (1.259) and therefore a larger mean distance from the ensemble mean to the truth. The surrogates, being imperfect models, may partially *compress* the chaotic divergence, leading to artificially lower fRMSE in the short run — but this apparent advantage disappears under longer forecasts (see Benchmark 2).

**Spread/fRMSE calibration:** The ideal ratio is 1.0. Lorenz63 achieves 0.933 (slightly under-dispersive, acceptable). All surrogates exceed 1.0, meaning their ensemble is *over-dispersive* relative to their own forecast error. LSTMNN (1.205) is the best calibrated surrogate. This over-dispersion is benign for DA: it prevents filter divergence and ensures the Kalman gain assigns appropriate weight to observations.

**RNN_relu** produces the highest aRMSE (0.673), though the difference is small in this setting. Its pattern of relative underperformance becomes clearer in the stress tests below.

**No model diverged at any M value.**

### 3.3 Observations

**Short to medium forecast windows (M ≤ 20, equivalent to 0–0.2 Lorenz time units):** Lorenz63 has the best aRMSE and LSTMNN is the top surrogate. Surrogates are 5–25% worse than the reference. All models maintain spread well.

**M = 40–50 (approaching the predictability horizon):** A significant bifurcation occurs. The Lorenz63 aRMSE grows dramatically (0.862 → 2.026) while the surrogates grow much more slowly (0.857–1.528). The surrogates' **lower sensitivity to forecast window length** is not because they are better models — it is because their learned dynamics are *less chaotic*. The Lorenz solver faithfully amplifies initial perturbations; the surrogate models partially dampen the divergence. This is visible in the spread tables: Lorenz63 produces more spread at longer windows, which correctly reflects the true predictability limits of the system, while the surrogates underestimate the chaotic growth.

**M = 100 (one full Lorenz time unit, near predictability limit):** Lorenz63 aRMSE = 2.221, which is close to the climatological error of the L63 attractor. The surrogates range from 2.349 (ResDenseNN) to 3.606 (RNN_relu). The best surrogate (ResDenseNN) is only marginally worse than the true solver, while RNN_relu degrades to 62% higher error. The ordering is: ResDenseNN ≈ Lorenz63 < RNN_tanh < LSTMNN < DenseNN << RNN_relu.

**Key finding:** RNN_relu's poor performance at long forecast windows is consistent with the ReLU activation potentially causing instability in the recurrent propagation. The tanh-gated architectures (RNN_tanh, LSTMNN) show more stable long-range behavior, with bounded activations preventing runaway error accumulation.

## 4. Benchmark 3 — Ensemble Size (Ne) Sensitivity

**Configuration:** T=50 cycles, M=10, p=1.0, σ=1.0; Ne swept over {5, 10, 20, 50}.

| Model | Ne=5 | Ne=10 | Ne=20 | Ne=50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| **DenseNN** | **0.409** | 0.393 | 0.373 | 0.376 |
| ResDenseNN | 0.431 | 0.388 | 0.386 | 0.383 |
| LSTMNN | 0.496 | 0.370 | **0.361** | 0.387 |
| RNN_relu | 0.689 | 0.531 | 0.482 | 0.482 |
| RNN_tanh | 0.416 | 0.405 | 0.404 | 0.385 |

### 4.2 Mean Spread by Model and Ensemble Size

| Model | Ne=5 | Ne=10 | Ne=20 | Ne=50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lorenz63 | 0.424 | 0.510 | 0.529 | 0.535 |
| DenseNN | 0.535 | 0.557 | 0.555 | 0.544 |
| ResDenseNN | 0.438 | 0.537 | 0.548 | 0.563 |
| LSTMNN | 0.423 | 0.484 | 0.508 | 0.531 |
| RNN_relu | 0.442 | 0.533 | 0.528 | 0.545 |
| RNN_tanh | 0.459 | 0.499 | 0.539 | 0.544 |

### 4.3 Observations

**Small ensemble (Ne=5) is the hardest setting** for all models, as expected — severe sampling noise degrades the covariance estimate. However, the impact is unequal across architectures.

The most striking result is at **Ne=5**: DenseNN (aRMSE=0.409) and RNN_tanh (0.416) *outperform the Lorenz63 baseline* (0.517). Lorenz63 is more sensitive to small ensembles, likely because it produces larger spread (0.424 vs 0.535 for DenseNN), and with Ne=5 members the sample covariance is very noisy relative to the ensemble variance. The surrogates' slightly over-dispersive character (broader spread relative to fRMSE) gives the ETKF analysis update a more "regularized" covariance, mitigating sampling noise effects.

**RNN_relu** is again the worst performer at every Ne, with aRMSE=0.689 at Ne=5 — 33% worse than Lorenz63. Its spread (0.442) is appropriate but the fRMSE is too high relative to other models, indicating that this architecture simply produces poorer short-range predictions.

Beyond Ne=10, all models converge to similar performance levels, with the hierarchy ResDenseNN ≈ LSTMNN ≈ Lorenz63 ≈ DenseNN < RNN_tanh < RNN_relu persisting. The **ensemble size has diminishing returns past Ne=10** for this 3-dimensional system.

## 5. Benchmark 4 — Observation Coverage (p) Sensitivity

**Configuration:** T=50, M=10, Ne=20, σ=1.0; p swept over {1.0 (3 obs), 0.67 (2 obs), 0.33 (1 obs)}.

|---|---|---|---|

| ResDenseNN | 0.386 | 0.818 | 1.914 |
| LSTMNN | 0.361 | 0.770 | 1.051 |
| RNN_relu | 0.482 | 0.695 | 1.533 |
| **RNN_tanh** | 0.404 | 0.708 | **0.895** |

### 5.2 Mean Ensemble Spread by Model and Observation Fraction

| Model | p=1.0 | p=0.67 | p=0.33 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lorenz63 | 0.529 | 0.680 | 1.120 |
| DenseNN | 0.555 | 0.683 | 1.108 |
| ResDenseNN | 0.548 | 0.721 | 1.175 |
| LSTMNN | 0.508 | 0.663 | 1.104 |
| RNN_relu | 0.528 | 0.669 | 1.080 |
| RNN_tanh | 0.539 | 0.688 | 1.067 |

### 5.3 Observations

**Full observation (p=1.0):** All models perform comparably, as established in previous benchmarks.

**Two observations (p=0.67):** All models degrade, but the relative ordering changes significantly. RNN_relu (0.695) and RNN_tanh (0.708) are now *better* than Lorenz63 (0.744). ResDenseNN (0.818) and DenseNN (0.822) are the worst surrogates. This suggests the memoryless MLP architectures suffer more when partial observations leave the state under-constrained, while the recurrent architectures' internal memory provides implicit regularization of the unobserved dimensions.

**Single observation (p=0.33, 1 out of 3 variables):** This is a severe under-determined problem for L63. The most remarkable result in the entire benchmark suite: **RNN_tanh achieves aRMSE=0.895, outperforming Lorenz63 (1.405) by 36%.** LSTMNN (1.051) and DenseNN (1.066) also outperform the reference model.

The spread values at p=0.33 are similar across all models (1.067–1.175), indicating that the performance differences stem from *forecast model dynamics*, not from spread differences. The recurrent architectures (RNN_tanh, LSTMNN) and even DenseNN appear to have learned dynamics that are more amenable to correction from a single observation. This could reflect a smoothing or compression of the chaotic attractor that, while physically inaccurate, makes the ensemble easier to steer back to the true trajectory.

**ResDenseNN at p=0.33 (aRMSE=1.914) is the single worst result in the benchmark**, even worse than Lorenz63. The residual connections do not appear to help under sparse observations and may in fact create a stronger attractor basin that resists single-observation corrections.

### 6.3 Observations

**High-quality observations (σ=0.1):** This is the setting that most clearly reveals **model error**. When observations are nearly perfect, only an accurate forecast model can take full advantage of them. Lorenz63 achieves aRMSE=0.036 — essentially tracking the truth. ResDenseNN (0.062) is the second-best, nearly 2× worse. The others range from 0.130 (DenseNN) to 0.433 (RNN_relu), a 12× spread in performance. **RNN_relu fails catastrophically** at σ=0.1, with aRMSE=0.433 barely improved from its σ=3.0 value of 1.338. This suggests that with tight observations, RNN_relu is unable to steer the ensemble toward the truth — the strong gradient of the ETKF update combined with RNN_relu's numerically stiff dynamics may cause inconsistent corrections.

**Moderate noise (σ=0.5–1.0):** The field compresses and all models perform similarly. LSTMNN is the best surrogate at σ=1.0 (0.361 vs 0.376 for Lorenz63).

**Noisy observations (σ=2.0–3.0):** A reversal again occurs. At σ=3.0, the ranking is RNN_relu (1.338) < ResDenseNN (1.485) < RNN_tanh (1.399) < DenseNN (1.525) < LSTMNN (1.634) ≈ **Lorenz63 (1.639)**. The true solver now performs *worst* among all models. Under very noisy observations, the ETKF provides only weak corrections, and the forecast quality determines the outcome. The surrogates' tendency to produce smoother, less chaotic trajectories becomes beneficial: they drift less between observations and are therefore easier to correct when the corrective signal is weak.

**Spread at high σ:** All models converge to similar spread values at σ≥2.0 (0.964–0.997 at σ=2.0), indicating that observation noise sets a floor on ensemble spread that all architectures respect.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 Spread-Dispersion Behavior and DA Compatibility

A key prerequisite for successful ensemble DA is that the ensemble must have sufficient spread to encompass the true state. A *collapsed* ensemble (all members converged to the same trajectory) renders the Kalman gain near-zero, causing filter divergence. **None of the tested architectures exhibited this failure**, even without inflation.

The over-dispersion of all surrogates (spread/fRMSE ≥ 1.0 in baseline) is architecturally explained:
- Imperfect models amplify initial perturbations differently from the true dynamics, often more randomly
- The learned mapping f_θ(x) has residuals that introduce variance not present in the reference solver
- Recurrent architectures carry hidden state uncertainty between steps, compounding the spread

While over-dispersion means the Kalman gain is slightly miscalibrated (over-weighting observations), this is preferable to the alternative of collapse. In operational settings, mild over-dispersion can be corrected with observation error inflation rather than requiring complex model adjustments.

### 7.2 Architecture Rankings

**Consistency across benchmarks:**

| Architecture | Consistent strengths | Consistent weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **LSTMNN** | Best/near-best at moderate M and Ne; well-calibrated spread | Degrades faster under very tight observations |
| **RNN_tanh** | Most resilient to sparse observations; best at p=0.33 | Slightly higher fRMSE in baseline |
| **ResDenseNN** | Best surrogate at long M (100); second-best at low σ | Catastrophic failure under p=0.33 |
| **DenseNN** | Good performance at small Ne; robust baseline | Mid-tier at most settings |
| **RNN_relu** | Best at high σ_obs (3.0); gains from noisy regime | Systematically worst at low σ and high Ne; worst at long M |

**Global ranking (averaged across conditions):** LSTMNN ≈ RNN_tanh > ResDenseNN ≈ DenseNN >> RNN_relu

### 7.3 Why Do Surrogates Sometimes Beat the True Solver?

This counterintuitive result appears in three regimes:
1. **Long forecast windows (M≥50):** Surrogates' smoother dynamics avoid the full chaotic divergence, causing lower ensemble error in the mean — but this reflects a compression of variance, not better prediction.
2. **Sparse observations (p=0.33):** The DA system has limited information to constrain the state. Surrogates whose dynamics project the state into a more constrained subspace are easier to correct with one observation.
3. **Noisy observations (σ=3.0):** Weak Kalman gains give the model dynamics time to dominate. Smoother surrogate dynamics accumulate less error between cycles.

In all three cases, the surrogate's "advantage" is conditional on the observation network being limited. With a dense, accurate observation network, the true solver is always preferable. This has practical implications: **the benefit of surrogate models in DA is largest when observational constraints are weakest.**

### 7.4 The Role of Activation Functions in Recurrent Architectures

The RNN_relu vs RNN_tanh comparison isolates the effect of activation function on DA performance. RNN_tanh consistently outperforms RNN_relu except at the highest noise levels. The reasons are:

1. **Bounded activations:** tanh keeps hidden state values in [-1, 1], preventing exploding activation norms during long rollouts
2. **Smoother gradients:** tanh has more uniform gradient flow compared to ReLU's dead neuron problem
3. **Better spread calibration:** RNN_tanh's spread/fRMSE ratio is 1.479 vs RNN_relu's 1.378 in baseline — but RNN_tanh has lower absolute fRMSE, indicating it produces both better forecasts and appropriate variance

The LSTM's gating mechanisms (input gate, forget gate, output gate) provide additional control over information flow through time, explaining why LSTMNN outperforms both vanilla RNN variants on most metrics.

### 7.5 Practical Implications for Geoscientific Applications

The proposal motivated this study with the challenge of replacing expensive numerical solvers in ensemble DA. The results suggest:

1. **All tested architectures are viable priors for ETKF** — no filter collapse observed, all maintain useful spread levels
2. **For operational use, inflation will likely be needed** to correct the over-dispersion, but only at modest levels
3. **Architecture choice matters most when observations are limited** — in well-observed systems, the choice of surrogate has less impact
4. **LSTM and tanh-RNN are the recommended architectures** for surrogate-based ensemble DA: they balance forecast accuracy with robust spread maintenance
5. **MLP variants (DenseNN, ResDenseNN) are simpler and competitive** at moderate settings, making them attractive for large-scale applications where computational cost of recurrent evaluation matters
6. **ReLU-activated RNNs should be avoided** in this setting due to instability under tight observations and long forecast windows

## 8. Conclusions

This study provides a controlled evaluation of five ML surrogate architectures as forward models in a deterministic ETKF applied to the Lorenz-63 system, with **no inflation and no localization** — the strictest test of raw surrogate quality.

**Primary conclusions:**

1. **No architecture caused filter divergence** across 5 benchmarks and a wide parameter sweep. All surrogates maintain ensemble spread sufficient for stable DA cycling. This is an encouraging result for the field.

2. **All surrogates are over-dispersive** (spread/fRMSE > 1.0 in baseline). LSTMNN is the best-calibrated surrogate (ratio 1.205), closest to the ideal. This property is benign: it provides intrinsic protection against variance collapse.

3. **LSTMNN and RNN_tanh are the top-performing surrogates** overall. They combine competitive short-range accuracy with robust behavior under stress conditions, particularly long forecast windows and sparse observations.

4. **RNN_relu is consistently the weakest architecture** across benchmarks, particularly at high M and low σ_obs. The unbounded ReLU activation leads to poor stability in recurrent rollouts and an inability to benefit from high-quality observations.

5. **Surrogate models can outperform the true solver** when observations are limited (sparse, noisy, or infrequent). This regime-dependent advantage is not evidence of superior dynamics but of a favorable bias-variance trade-off under weak observational constraint.

6. **ResDenseNN is the most polarized architecture**: excellent at long forecast windows, but catastrophically poor under 1-observation settings (p=0.33, aRMSE=1.914 vs 1.405 for Lorenz63). It carries high risk for operational deployment without careful observational network assessment.

7. **The forecast window M is the most discriminating parameter** for ranking architectures. All models are similar at M≤20, but strongly differentiated at M≥50. The transition happens near the Lorenz-63 Lyapunov time (~1.1 Lorenz time units = M≈110 at dt=0.01), consistent with theoretical expectations.

**Open questions for future work:**
- Can covariance inflation specifically tuned per architecture recover the lost calibration?
- Does the over-dispersion remain stable over thousands of DA cycles, or does it drift?
- How do these rankings generalize to higher-dimensional chaotic systems (Lorenz-96, operational NWP models)?
- Can architecture search or training regularization be used to explicitly optimize the spread/fRMSE ratio?
